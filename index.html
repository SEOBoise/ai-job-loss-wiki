    Statistics, jobs at risk, and what the evidence shows about AI and work.

    AI job loss describes positions eliminated, hours reduced or opportunities to enter work diminished as artificial intelligence changes the demand for human labor. It can involve an employer automating a task, reorganizing a team or choosing not to replace departing workers. It is different from AI exposure: a judgment about which tasks a technology could affect.

    The evidence needs several lenses. Announcements record employers’ stated plans. Payroll and household data measure employment outcomes. Exposure indexes assess potential task change, while forecasts describe possible futures. None is a substitute for all the others. The research below contains warning signs for some workers and limited evidence of broad disruption in other measures; the differences depend on the population, period and question being studied.

    The focus is the United States, with international findings identified separately. Every statistical claim retains its date and scope. The evidence section addresses what is being observed; the occupation matrix explains how to assess the work behind a job title.

    What does AI job loss mean?

    A job is a bundle of activities, relationships and responsibilities. An employer may automate a recurring activity while retaining the person who handles exceptions, coordinates colleagues or makes accountable decisions. Alternatively, removing enough work can reduce the need for a role. The distinction matters because a demonstration of task capability is not a measurement of employment change.

    Layoffs, hiring and employment are different measures

    The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics’ Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS) treats hires as additions to payroll and separations as departures. Separations include quits, layoffs and discharges, and other departures. Openings describe positions available to fill under the survey’s criteria. These measures capture different stages of employer demand; an advertised vacancy is not a completed hire.¹

    The Current Population Survey (CPS) classifies people by employment and labor-force status. In general, unemployed people have no job, are available for work and have actively looked recently; people on temporary layoff can qualify without searching. A person who is not working but has stopped searching can instead be outside the labor force. That is why a decline in hiring does not automatically produce an equal increase in the unemployment rate.²

    The practical question is not only whether a system can produce an output. It is whether the complete service can be delivered reliably, at acceptable cost, with someone responsible for mistakes. That question connects technical capability to adoption, workflow redesign and labor demand.

    AI job loss statistics: what current evidence shows

    There is no single, harmonized causal count of worldwide AI job losses in the evidence reviewed here. A responsible comparison begins with what each source actually observes. The following research includes employer announcements, an administrative payroll sample, broader labor-market tracking and linked workplace records. Its findings should not be combined into a universal displacement percentage.

    Job-cut announcements citing AI

    Challenger, Gray & Christmas recorded 116,175 announced job cuts citing AI among 529,914 total announced cuts by U.S.-based employers during January-August 2026. In August alone, AI was cited in 3,462 of 52,881 announced cuts. The September 3 release describes plans, some of which cover multiple years, rather than a verified causal count of completed layoffs or unemployed people.³

    Employment and hiring evidence from Stanford

    A Stanford Digital Economy Lab working paper, revised August 12, 2026, uses ADP payroll data through June 2026. It reports employment for workers ages 22-25 in AI-exposed occupations 19% below a path matching less-exposed peers, with the divergence driven mainly by reduced hiring. The authors describe the results as early descriptive signals, not causal estimates. They report no widespread economy-wide displacement and note weaker patterns after education controls, some earlier divergent trends and differences between their sample and national benchmarks.⁴

    Broad tracking from Yale

    The Budget Lab at Yale’s tracker, updated September 15, 2026, does not find a clear AI-related labor-market footprint in the measures it examines. Its occupational-mix, usage and exposure analyses do not establish a broad disruption signal. That conclusion concerns those measures and their available data; it does not prove that every employer, occupation or subgroup is unaffected.⁵

    Earnings, hours and workplace change in Denmark

    Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard link adoption surveys to Danish administrative records in a March 2026 discussion paper. Their late-2024 survey covers about 25,000 workers across 7,000 workplaces in 11 exposed occupations. With outcome records through December 2024, they estimate average effects near zero on earnings and recorded hours, ruling out effects larger than 2%. They also document task reorganization. The observation window, country and selected occupations limit how far this result can be generalized.⁶

    An announcement count, a relative employment gap and an estimated effect on earnings have different units and denominators. A disagreement between their headlines may reflect those differences rather than a direct contradiction. The first step in interpretation is to identify the population and outcome before asking which result is more persuasive.

    AI exposure is not the same as job loss

    Occupational exposure estimates where AI capabilities overlap with the tasks people perform. It is a way to locate possible change, not an insurance-style probability that an individual will be dismissed. A highly exposed occupation can contain tasks that are automated, tasks that become more valuable and tasks that are difficult to change.

    ILO and NASK: generative AI task exposure

    The International Labour Organization and Poland’s NASK estimate that 25% of global employment is in occupations with some exposure to generative AI, rising to 34% in high-income countries. Their May 2025 study combines task information, expert input and model-assisted assessment. Its central distinction is between potential exposure and realized job losses; many affected roles may be transformed rather than removed.⁷

    IMF: a broader AI exposure framework

    The International Monetary Fund’s January 2024 analysis estimates that almost 40% of global employment is exposed to AI, with about 60% in advanced economies, 40% in emerging markets and 26% in low-income countries. It considers both replacement and complementarity. These figures use a different framework and technology boundary from the ILO’s GenAI assessment; they are neither a later observation of the same series nor counts of jobs destroyed.⁸

    Why exposure indexes can disagree

    Yale’s February 2026 comparison finds that exposure measures broadly agree but diverge more over highly exposed occupations. Differences often concern the magnitude of exposure rather than whether an occupation is touched by AI at all. An index is therefore best read alongside its definition: the tasks included, the capability threshold and the way tasks are combined into occupations.⁹

    For example, an index might ask whether a tool can shorten a task, while another places more weight on observed automated usage. Those questions are useful for different purposes. Neither directly supplies adoption cost, customer acceptance, how frequently the task occurs or what share of a person’s paid time it consumes. A rank in an exposure list is only the beginning of an assessment.

    A useful personal assessment starts with the task rather than the title. Ask what the tool would need as input, whether the output can be checked, who handles unusual cases and whether the organization has a reason to adopt it. The same occupation can look different across employers because its task mix and operating constraints differ.

    Which jobs and tasks are exposed to AI?

    The O*NET occupational information system describes work through tasks, skills, activities and other characteristics. This task-based approach is more informative than treating a familiar job title as a single activity. It also helps distinguish occupations from industries: an accountant in a hospital and an accountant in a technology company can share tasks while operating in different sectors.¹⁰

    The examples below are an explanatory matrix, not an empirical ranking or a forecast of how many jobs will disappear. They show where assistance might enter a workflow and what a complete evaluation would still need to address. They do not establish that every employer already uses these tools or that a particular system meets professional requirements.

    Clerical work and customer service

    Consider two apparently similar activities: classifying a routine document and resolving a disputed account. The first may have a clear label and a repeatable checking procedure. The second may depend on evidence outside the document and on the authority to decide an exception. Counting both simply as “office work” hides the difference. A task inventory should record complexity, frequency, verification and who is responsible when a decision is challenged.

    Programming, analysis and professional judgment

    Large language models can generate text or code, but producing a plausible draft is not the same as completing a reliable professional service. In an illustrative software workflow, generating a function is separate from deciding what the product should do, testing interactions, protecting user information and maintaining the result. A financial summary similarly needs correct records, explicit assumptions and review. These distinctions help explain why exposure can be high without establishing that an entire role is redundant.

    Physical work and interpersonal responsibilities

    A text-based GenAI assessment should not be treated as a complete assessment of robotics, machinery or other automation. Physical and interpersonal roles can also include substantial documentation and scheduling. Conversely, a system that assists with those administrative tasks may do little to replace the physical service. “Less exposed in this framework” is more precise than “AI-proof.” Technology boundaries, adoption conditions and changing demand remain relevant.

    AI and entry-level jobs: hiring versus layoffs

    For a new entrant, a vacancy that is never created can matter as much as an existing position being eliminated. It can change the route into paid work and the opportunity to learn through supervised tasks. That is different from saying every decline in junior hiring is caused by AI: the business cycle, earlier hiring decisions, occupational composition and other changes can also affect recruitment.

    What research measures about young workers

    Anthropic’s March 2026 analysis combines theoretical LLM capability, platform usage and U.S. labor surveys. It finds no systematic unemployment increase in highly exposed occupations, alongside a tentative 14% decline in the job-finding rate for ages 22-25 entering exposed work, relative to the 2022 baseline. That estimate is only barely statistically significant. Alternative explanations and transition-measurement issues remain. This is vendor-authored research with selective platform coverage, not an all-employer census.¹¹

    The Stanford result discussed earlier and the Anthropic measure concern related questions but different outcomes. Employment levels and a job-finding rate are not interchangeable. Nor are “ages 22-25,” “recent graduates” and “entry-level jobs.” A young person can have substantial experience, an older person can be changing careers, and many new entrants are not graduates. Keeping the labels precise prevents an important subgroup finding from becoming an unsupported claim about everyone beginning a career.

    Hiring and opportunities to learn

    A useful employer response is to examine where junior staff gain judgment. If routine drafting or initial analysis becomes faster, supervised review, customer context and responsibility for checking work can still be designed into an entry role. Removing every preparatory task without redesigning learning may leave a weaker route to experience. This is a practical design consideration, not a measured forecast of future skill shortages.

    For job seekers, evidence of verified work can be more informative than a list of tools used. An example portfolio might explain the original task, the assistance received, the checks performed and what was corrected. It should distinguish personal judgment from generated output and exclude confidential employer information. Such a portfolio demonstrates a process; it cannot guarantee an offer in a weak hiring market.

    Business AI adoption and productivity

    U.S. Census business-use statistics

    For the period ending May 3, 2026, the U.S. Census Bureau’s Business Trends and Outlook Survey reported AI use by 19.8% of businesses, compared with 39.7% in Information and 33.9% in Finance and Insurance. The question covers use in the preceding two weeks. In November 2025 it expanded from production of goods or services to any business function, limiting simple comparisons with the older wording.¹²

    A measured productivity gain in customer support

    In Generative AI at Work, Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond study 5,172 customer-support agents. The revised paper reports an average 15% increase in issues resolved per hour with AI assistance. Results vary across workers, with greater improvements among less experienced and lower-skilled agents. This is a specific workplace outcome; it does not establish a 15% staffing reduction, a universal wage increase or an economy-wide productivity effect.¹³

    A company considering deployment needs to measure more than speed. A faster first response may be less useful if it increases corrections, repeat contacts or unresolved problems. A sound comparison can record completed work, output quality, rework, review time and the experience of staff and customers. The appropriate metric depends on the service: counting drafts produced can be misleading when the real goal is an accurate decision or a resolved request.

    Adoption requires integration, review and operating choices

    An AI assistant may sit inside a human-led workflow. An AI agent may be given tools and discretion to carry out several steps. In either case, deployment requires decisions about data access, verification, escalation and responsibility. Buying access to a model is not equivalent to replacing a complete business process. Integration and ongoing supervision can introduce work as well as remove it.

    Adoption statistics also depend on the unit being counted. A firm may use AI in one small function while most employees never interact with it. A worker may use a personal tool in a company without a formal deployment. Those situations require different questions. Comparing a percentage of businesses with a percentage of workers without explaining the distinction produces an unreliable picture of how widespread a change is.

    How AI can reduce, change or create work

    The employment effect of a productivity improvement depends on what happens next. At a fixed amount of output, needing less labor time can reduce hours or staffing requirements. If lower cost or better service attracts more demand, the amount of work can expand. An organization can also reassign people, slow replacement hiring or build new services. These are possible mechanisms, not a claim that they occur in equal proportions.

    Automation, augmentation and workflow redesign

    Automation substitutes for an activity; augmentation helps a person perform work. A single deployment can do both. A hypothetical reporting tool might automate formatting while helping an analyst investigate an unusual result. The analyst’s time changes, but so may the number of reports requested. A complete assessment asks about the full workflow, including supervision and tasks created around the tool.

    Illustrative example: a support team

    An assistant drafts a response that an employee checks. The team could use the saved time to serve more customers, reduce overtime, slow hiring or move staff into complex cases. These are alternative possibilities in a fictional example, not reported staffing outcomes from the support-agent study.

    Demand expansion, new tasks and net employment

    New work can include evaluating outputs, integrating systems, managing exceptions and maintaining records of decisions. But the existence of new tasks does not establish the number of new jobs, and those jobs may require different skills or be located elsewhere. Counting both losses and gains matters; so does asking whether displaced workers can reach the opportunities that emerge.

    Consider a hypothetical industry that loses 100 roles in one activity and adds 120 in another. Net employment rises by 20, yet the original 100 workers may still face disruption. The new jobs may involve different pay, schedules, credentials or geography. A positive net total is therefore compatible with serious individual losses. The example illustrates arithmetic and adjustment costs, not an empirical AI forecast.

    Wages, hours and the distribution of gains

    Headcount alone can miss changes to paid hours, earnings, work intensity or opportunities for advancement. A worker might keep a job while performing a different mix of tasks. An employer might capture cost savings, share them through pay, invest in expansion or reduce prices. These alternatives depend on bargaining, demand, competition and organizational choices. They should be investigated rather than assumed from a tool’s speed.

    It is also possible for a measured gain in one task to disappear once review and integration are counted. A useful pilot tracks the complete time needed to reach an acceptable result. It should distinguish a gross time saving on a first draft from a net saving after correction. That accounting is a practical way to connect technical performance with the labor input an organization actually needs.

    What AI job forecasts can and cannot tell us

    The World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs Report 2025 projects 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced by 2030, for a net increase of 78 million. These estimates cover multiple structural trends, including technology, demographics and economic change. The same report estimates that 39% of existing skill sets will change or become outdated, and that 59 in 100 workers would need training by 2030. These are forward-looking estimates, not observed changes or AI-only totals.¹⁴

    A checklist for interpreting any future-of-work number
    Forecast questionWhy the answer matters
    What is the technology boundary?AI, generative AI and all forms of automation are different scopes.
    What year is the endpoint?A long-run scenario cannot be read as a count of current layoffs.
    Who supplied the expectations?Employer intentions, statistical projections and model scenarios have different foundations.
    Is it gross or net change?Displacement, creation and their balance should stay separate.
    Which assumptions could change?Adoption cost, demand, capability and institutions affect the pathway.

    Scenarios can be valuable without being predictions of a single inevitable future. They help expose which assumptions drive a result. For instance, a scenario built on fast adoption and little demand growth may imply different staffing effects from one with slower adoption and substantial expansion. A clear forecast states those conditions rather than presenting a distant endpoint as already established.

    The scale of retraining needs also should not be confused with the number of people expected to become unemployed. Training can support people who remain in their roles, move within an employer or change occupations. The cost, accessibility and practical relevance of that training matter. A course completion count alone does not show whether people found suitable work.

    Worker vulnerability, adaptation and public concern

    Exposure and adaptive capacity answer different questions

    A January 2026 Brookings analysis examines workers’ ability to manage a transition alongside AI exposure. Of 37.1 million U.S. workers in its highest exposure quartile, 26.5 million have above-median adaptive capacity. It identifies 6.1 million with high exposure and low adaptive capacity; about 86% of that group are women. Its framework considers liquid resources, age, geographic opportunity and skill transferability. These are vulnerability measures, not predictions that those workers will lose their jobs.¹⁵

    Two people doing similar work can face very different adjustment costs. One may have savings, transferable skills and several employers nearby. Another may face limited transport, care responsibilities or few suitable vacancies. The technical ability of a model to assist with a task does not describe those circumstances. A useful response considers both the work that may change and the practical resources available for a transition.

    Public expectations are not employment outcomes

    Pew Research Center’s June 22-28, 2026 survey finds that 71% of U.S. adults expect AI to lead to fewer U.S. jobs over the next 20 years; 5% expect more jobs and 10% expect little difference. The report was published August 18. These percentages measure public expectations. They should not be presented as a scientific probability of job loss or as the share of people already displaced.¹⁶

    Concern is understandable even when a broad labor-market measure appears stable. A person deciding what to study or whether to change roles needs information about access, pay and learning opportunities, not just an aggregate total. At the same time, a fearful survey response cannot identify the cause of an employment trend. Public opinion and labor-market evidence are important for different reasons.

    How workers and employers can prepare for AI-driven change

    Preparation is most useful when it produces specific evidence about tasks, skills and opportunities. It should not depend on a promise that a particular occupation is permanently safe or that a single certificate prevents displacement. The following steps are planning suggestions; they cannot remove the effects of a weak local labor market or an employer’s staffing decisions.

    Task assessment, upskilling and reskilling

    1. Inventory the work. List recurring tasks, how often they occur, the inputs required and the consequences of an error. Separate routine preparation from decisions that require context or responsibility.
    2. Test within approved boundaries. Use permitted tools and nonconfidential examples. Compare the complete process, including checking and correction, rather than only the time to produce an initial output.
    3. Identify adjacent skills. Upskilling strengthens capability in an existing role; reskilling prepares for different work. Investigate actual job requirements before paying for training.
    4. Document judgment. Keep examples of how you verified sources, found mistakes, handled exceptions or improved a process. Describe what you did, what the tool did and what the result demonstrates.
    5. Explore realistic pathways. Consider local vacancies, transferability, schedule, credentials and training costs. Internal redeployment and external moves have different constraints.

    The U.S. Department of Labor’s American Job Centers overview points to employment and training services. It is an official starting point for job-search and workforce assistance. Particular services and eligibility should be checked with the relevant local center; availability should not be assumed from a general description.¹⁷

    Redeployment, training and worker consultation

    Employers can start by defining what a pilot is meant to improve: quality, turnaround time, access or cost. Record a baseline and compare the complete workflow after deployment. Include employees who understand unusual cases and hidden coordination work. A task that looks easy in a process diagram may depend on information that is not written down.

    Specify who checks outputs and who can stop or reverse an automated action. Track rework and failure cases alongside successful completions. Decide how changes affect workload and learning opportunities before assuming that a faster task creates spare capacity. Where feasible, consider redeployment and paid training as part of the implementation plan, with clear expectations about responsibilities and assessment.

    Worker consultation is useful for identifying missing context and distributional effects. It can reveal whether one team saves time by shifting work onto another, or whether a tool helps experienced staff while making entry harder for new colleagues. These are questions to examine through the deployment process. They are not a claim about a universal legal requirement or a guarantee that every rollout can preserve every position.

    How to evaluate an AI job-loss claim

    A strong claim names the outcome, the population, the observation period and the comparison. “AI affects work” is broad enough to include many different events. “Employment in a defined exposed group changed relative to another group during a stated period” is narrower and easier to test. Clear boundaries make research more useful, even when its conclusion is uncertain.

    Association, counterfactuals and confounding factors

    A counterfactual is the comparison with what would have happened without the change being studied. It cannot normally be observed directly, so researchers use designs and assumptions to approximate it. Confounding factors are other influences that may affect the comparison. Interest rates, demand, composition and earlier hiring patterns are examples worth investigating; naming them is not proof that they explain a result.

    A difference-in-differences design compares changes across groups over time. Its interpretation depends on the design and assumptions, including whether the groups would have followed comparable paths without the intervention. The label alone does not establish causation. Readers should examine preexisting trends, group definitions, sample coverage and the uncertainty around an estimate.

    Percentages, percentage points and relative gaps

    Illustrative arithmetic

    A rate falling from 10% to 8% declines by 2 percentage points, or by 20% relative to its original level. A team that stays at 100 people while a comparison team grows to 110 has slower growth; that does not mean it laid off 10 workers. These fictional examples show why the unit and comparison must stay attached to a number.

    Check whether a chart is counting people, jobs, businesses, tasks or announcements. Then ask whether the data are observed, self-reported, modeled or projected. Finally, check publication and observation dates separately. A paper released in 2026 can study outcomes ending in 2024. The more prominent the statistic, the closer its explanation and source should be.

    Frequently asked questions about AI job loss

    Is AI already replacing jobs?

    Employers have attributed some announced reductions to AI, and researchers examine changes in hiring and employment among exposed groups. Those are different kinds of evidence. A specific announcement can support a statement about an employer’s plans without proving AI’s causal share of all job losses.

    How many jobs have been lost because of AI?

    The sources here do not provide one harmonized causal global total. The announcement figures in the evidence section describe a defined tracker and period. Exposure percentages and projected displacement totals should not be added to those figures or relabeled as completed layoffs.

    Does high AI exposure mean my job will disappear?

    No. Exposure concerns overlap between tasks and capabilities. Outcomes depend on actual adoption, verification needs, demand and how an organization redesigns work. It is a reason to investigate a task mix, not a probability of dismissal for an individual.

    Why are entry-level jobs a concern?

    Slower hiring can narrow routes into paid work and experience before it shows up clearly in layoffs. The relevant research uses specific samples and age groups. It should not be generalized to every graduate, all young adults or everyone beginning a new career.

    Can AI create jobs as well as eliminate them?

    New tasks and demand growth are possible channels for job creation. They do not guarantee that displaced workers can reach the new roles. Net growth, the quality of work and individual transition costs are separate questions.

    Is any job completely safe from AI?

    An absolute safety guarantee is not supported. A role may have limited exposure in a particular GenAI framework while still experiencing changes in administration, demand or other automation. Assess the tasks, technology boundary and operating conditions rather than relying on a permanent safe-jobs list.

    Are forecasts the same as job-loss counts?

    No. A forecast is forward-looking and conditional. An announcement records a plan, and an employment statistic measures an outcome in a defined population. Their numbers can be useful together only when these distinctions remain visible.

    What should I do if AI changes my role?

    Start with a task and skills inventory, approved tool trials and realistic adjacent roles. Investigate employer training or redeployment and official workforce services. Compare the cost and relevance of training before committing, and avoid promises that one tool or course guarantees employment.

    Glossary of AI and employment terms

    Adaptive capacity
    The resources and opportunities that help a worker navigate a transition.
    AI adoption
    Actual integration and use of AI within a setting, distinct from possible capability.
    AI agent
    A system given tools and some discretion to carry out a sequence of tasks.
    Attrition
    Departures that may reduce staffing when positions are not refilled.
    Augmentation
    Assistance that changes how a person completes work.
    Automation
    A system performing an activity that otherwise requires human effort.
    Counterfactual
    The comparison with what would have occurred without the change being studied.
    Generative AI
    AI systems used to generate content such as text, images or code.
    Job-finding rate
    A measure of transitions into work for a specified group and interval.
    Labor demand
    The amount and type of work employers seek under particular conditions.
    Labor productivity
    Output relative to labor input; the specific outcome must be defined.
    Large language model
    A model used to process or generate language, including text and code.
    Net employment change
    The balance of job gains and losses within a defined boundary.
    Occupational exposure
    An assessment of how a technology overlaps with an occupation’s tasks.
    Reskilling
    Developing capabilities for different work.
    Upskilling
    Strengthening capabilities within an existing role or field.
